Home networking: the foundation your smart home stands on

The most common smart home fault is not a broken device — it is a network that cannot carry the load.

The correct shape is simple: ONT in bridge mode → main router → switch → access points with wired backhaul. If you own the property and can run cable during renovation, do not use wireless-backhaul Mesh. One rule handles most cases: if it can be wired, never make it wireless.

Separate three things#

  • WAN bandwidth — what your ISP sells you; affects downloads and streaming.
  • LAN quality — latency, packet loss and roaming; decides whether smart devices obey you.
  • Coverage density — number and placement of APs; decides whether there are dead zones.

Upgrading to gigabit only fixes the first. Smart homes mostly fail on the second and third.

Choosing a topology#

Topology and key devices#

  1. Bridge the ONT so the router handles PPPoE; disable the ONT's Wi-Fi to avoid double NAT and roaming confusion.
  2. Main router: dial-up, DHCP, VLANs, guest network. Lives in the service box.
  3. Switch: pick a port count and leave 30% headroom. PoE powers ceiling APs and cameras.
  4. APs: ceiling units for living rooms and corridors, wall units for bedrooms. Same brand and series, so roaming behaves consistently.

Always isolate IoT on its own network#

  • Smart devices get a separate SSID, 2.4 GHz only, separate VLAN.
  • Phones, laptops and TVs stay on the 5 GHz main network.
  • Benefits: fewer broadcast storms, no unwanted scanning of your devices, cleaner Wi-Fi 6 scheduling.
  • Legacy 2.4 GHz-only devices connect reliably because the SSID is not dual-band.

Acceptance criteria you can run#

  • Per room: signal ≥ −65 dBm, zero packet loss.
  • Voice control while walking from living room to bedroom: handover under 100 ms, no dropouts.
  • Wired points: all 8 conductors pass, negotiated at 1 Gbps or better.
  • 24-hour soak test: continuous ping with no more than three lost packets.

Never accept a speed test taken in the living room only. Smart home complaints come from bedrooms, bathrooms and balconies.

Common dead zones#

  • Bathrooms — masonry plus tile reflections; use the corridor AP or add a wall unit.
  • Bedside in the main bedroom — wall APs hidden behind nightstands; raise them or go ceiling-mounted.
  • Open kitchens — metal cabinets and hoods; keep APs off the metal path.
  • Balcony and entry — camera and lock gateway needs; verify separately.

Still have questions?

Is wireless-backhaul Mesh really that bad?

It works with conditions: node-to-node signal of −60 dBm or better and no more than three nodes. Each wireless hop roughly halves throughput and handovers stutter. If you have cable, use wired backhaul.

More APs are better, right?

No. Too many APs cause interference and ping-pong handovers. One AP per two to three rooms, with transmit power kept low to medium, is the sweet spot.

What router do I need for gigabit fibre?

A 2.5 GbE main router, CAT6A cabling and Wi-Fi 6/7 APs. The bottleneck is usually the switch or old cabling, not the internet connection.
