Rough-in wiring: 12 decisions to lock in at the electrical stage

After the walls are closed, wiring is the only thing that cannot be fixed cheaply. Print this list and hand it to your electrician.

Four things decide half your outcome: a neutral wire in every switch box, power inside every curtain box, enough network points on every wall, and a larger service box with four sockets. Together they add roughly ¥3,000–8,000 — and save you a demolition job.

Rule of thumb: decide everything that gets buried in a wall now; everything that sits on a surface can wait.

Switch boxes: the neutral wire is the entry ticket#

  1. Neutral (N) in every switch box, including two-way positions — nearly all smart switches are neutral-required.
  2. Back box depth: use 60 mm; smart modules are thicker than mechanical switches.
  3. Gangs: living room and entry benefit from 4–6 gang positions, or one switch plus a scene panel.
  4. Two-way circuits: do not seal off the spare traveller — reuse it for a multi-way panel or sensor power.

Do not wire heater-blower units, fan heaters, motors or LED drivers with large capacitors directly to a smart switch. Use a smart panel with a dry-contact or relay module instead — otherwise expect flicker or hardware failure.

Curtains: power location decides everything#

  • Put the socket inside the top of the curtain box, 100–200 mm from the ceiling, on the motor side, facing the wall.
  • Double-track electric curtain boxes: net width ≥ 200 mm, depth ≥ 150 mm.
  • Bay windows, L-shaped and curved windows need bendable or split tracks — confirm during measurement.
  • Leave an access hatch; never seal the box completely.

Network: cable and points#

  1. CAT6A U/UTP is enough for a decade. Do not compromise on Cat5e.
  2. TV positions: 2–3 cables each (IPTV, console, spare).
  3. AP points: one per bedroom, living room and corridor — more points, lower transmit power later.
  4. Entry: one cable each for the door lock gateway, video doorbell and outdoor camera (PoE for cameras).
  5. Label every cable at both ends with room and purpose.

The service box is the heart of the system#

  • Size: at least 500×400×120 mm for ONT, main router, switch and NAS power supplies.
  • Four sockets inside (or a 4–6 way power strip) on a dedicated breaker.
  • Cooling: metal enclosure with vents; move high-power switches into a small wall cabinet.
  • Location: never behind shoe cabinets, sofas or wardrobes — you must be able to open the door.

Door locks, sensors and HVAC#

  • Door lock: reserve a cable in the frame or choose a battery model; run the door sensor cable to the frame head.
  • Presence sensors: reserve 220V-to-5V or PoE in ceilings; mmWave sensors work best ceiling-mounted.
  • Water and gas sensors: sockets plus sensor positions under kitchen sinks, near water heaters and in bathrooms.
  • Air conditioning, floor heating, fresh air: check for Modbus/RS485 or dry contacts; wire it rather than using IR where possible.

How to verify#

  • Every switch box: confirm neutral wire with a multimeter.
  • Every network point: test all 8 conductors and record the label.
  • Every curtain box: socket live, position within track range.
  • Service box: socket count and temperature after one hour of operation.
  • Photograph everything before the walls close — you will need it later.

Still have questions?

Can I use smart switches in an old home without neutral wires?

Three options: re-run neutrals (most disruptive, most thorough), use single-live-wire smart switches (minimum load limits, flicker risk), or keep mechanical switches and put the intelligence in the fixtures — smart bulbs or relay modules with wireless wall panels.

Can network and power cables share a conduit?

Standards call for separate conduits at least 200 mm apart, crossing perpendicular where unavoidable. If they must share a chase, use shielded cable with proper grounding — but separating them is usually cheap.

Should I reserve all this even if I am not installing smart devices now?

Yes. Rough-in adds roughly 2–4% to a hard-fit budget and preserves your options. Think of it as buying upgrade capability, not buying devices early.
